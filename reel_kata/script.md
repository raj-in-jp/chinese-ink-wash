## Narration (English take with pauses; each Japanese word dropped into its pause — no cuts in speech)
Why does Japanese turn McDonald's into マクドナルド (Makudonarudo)? It's not a mistake. It's a rule.
Japanese words are built from small beats. And almost every beat ends in a vowel. か き く け こ (ka ki ku ke ko).
The only sound that can end without a vowel is "n". So when English puts consonants together, Japanese adds a little
vowel to each one. Test becomes テスト (tesuto). Christmas becomes クリスマス (Kurisumasu). Which vowel? Usually "u".
But after "t" and "d", it's "o". Some English sounds don't exist in Japanese at all. There's no separate L and R. Just
one sound in between. So rice and lice are both ライス (raisu). There's no "th", so thank you becomes サンキュー (sankyū).
And "v" often becomes "b". Violin is バイオリン (baiorin). So why is Paris パリ (Pari)? Because Japan borrowed it from
French, where the S is silent. From English, it would have been パリス (Parisu).
And Paris isn't alone. A lot of "weird English" in Japanese isn't English at all. Energy? エネルギー (enerugī). That's
from German, Energie. Allergy? アレルギー (arerugī). German too. Bread? パン (pan). From Portuguese, pão. Coffee?
コーヒー (kōhī). From Dutch, koffie. So when Japanese English sounds odd… it might not be English at all!

## Fact notes
- Japanese morae are (consonant +) vowel; the only coda is the moraic nasal ん (the small っ doubles the next consonant).
- Loanword epenthesis: usually u; o after t and d (テスト, ベッド); i after ch / j (マッチ, ブリッジ).
- One liquid /r/ for English l and r: rice and lice are both ライス.  No "th": s / z (サンキュー).  v often b (バイオリン);
  ヴ exists but everyday words mostly use b.
- パリ follows the French pronunciation of Paris (final s silent).
- エネルギー from German Energie; アレルギー from German Allergie; パン from Portuguese pão; コーヒー from Dutch koffie.
