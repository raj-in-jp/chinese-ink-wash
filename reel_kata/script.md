## Narration
Why does Japanese turn McDonald's into マクドナルド? It's not a mistake. It's a rule.
Japanese words are built from small beats. And almost every beat ends in a vowel. か, き, く, け, こ.
The only sound that can end without a vowel is "n".
So when English puts consonants together, Japanese adds a little vowel to each one.
Test becomes テスト. Christmas becomes クリスマス. Which vowel? Usually "u". But after "t" and "d", it's "o".
Some English sounds don't exist in Japanese at all. There's no separate L and R. Just one sound in between.
So rice and lice are both ライス. There's no "th", so thank you becomes サンキュー. And "v" often becomes "b".
Violin is バイオリン.
So why is Paris パリ? Because Japan borrowed it from French, where the S is silent. From English, it would have been
パリス. Follow for more. またね！

## Fact notes
- Japanese morae are (consonant +) vowel; the only coda is the moraic nasal ん (the small っ doubles the next consonant).
- Loanword epenthesis: usually u; o after t and d (テスト, ベッド); i after ch / j (マッチ, ブリッジ).
- One liquid /r/ for English l and r: rice and lice are both ライス.  No "th": s / z (サンキュー).  v often b (バイオリン);
  ヴ exists but everyday words mostly use b.
- パリ follows the French pronunciation of Paris (final s silent).
