## v2 narration (recorded; Scribe word-exact, English and Japanese)

No history of how the kana were made (that was an earlier reel): each script's job, and why mixing them helps.

Japanese uses three alphabets... in one sentence! Why?!
Look. 私はコーヒーを飲みます。 "I drink coffee." Kanji. Hiragana. Katakana. All in one line.
          [hook: 3 ALPHABETS slams in, the sentence's characters fly in, steaming cup, WHY?!; each script hops as named]
And each one has a job.
Kanji carries the meaning. わたし, "I". のむ, "drink".              [私 card with a figure, 飲 card with the cup]
Hiragana handles the grammar. は, を, and the polite ending, ます.    [は を ます hop: TOPIC / OBJECT / POLITE]
Katakana writes foreign words. コーヒー comes from the Dutch word for coffee.   [koffie (Dutch flag) → コーヒー]
But why mix all three? Because Japanese has no spaces. Write it all in hiragana, and it's a wall of letters. Mix the
three, and every time the script changes, your eye sees a new word.  [NO SPACES!, the hiragana wall, dividers drop]
So, which one did you learn first? Hiragana, katakana, or kanji? Tell me in the comments, and follow for more. またね！

## Fact notes

- は (topic marker, read wa) and を (object marker, read o) are particles; ます is the polite verb ending.
- コーヒー comes from Dutch koffie (brought by Dutch traders in the Edo period).
- A change of script is a strong hint of a word boundary, not a rule (飲 | みます is one word); the dividers are drawn
  between words only.
