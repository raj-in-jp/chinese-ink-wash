## Narration (recorded; Scribe word-exact)

Japanese uses three alphabets... in one sentence. Why?!
Look at this. 私はコーヒーを飲みます。 "I drink coffee." This part is kanji. This is hiragana. And this, is katakana.
Three scripts, in one sentence.                                              [hook: the sentence lights up script by script]
About fifteen hundred years ago, Japan had no writing. So it borrowed Chinese characters: kanji.   [sea chart, 漢字 sail over]
But there was a problem. Chinese words never change their endings. Japanese words do. Taberu, eat. Tabeta, ate.
Tabenai, don't eat. Kanji alone couldn't write those endings.            [食べる 食べた 食べない, the endings become ?]
The fix? Scribes wrote kanji faster and faster, in flowing cursive, until the shapes softened. An, became a. That's
hiragana. And it writes the grammar, and the word endings.              [安 → written fast → あ; the verbs come back]
Then Buddhist monks took just one piece of a kanji, like the left side of i, and made katakana. Today, it writes foreign
words, like kōhī, coffee.                                                 [伊 → 亻 → イ; コーヒー]
And here's the payoff. Japanese has no spaces. But kanji carries the meaning, hiragana the grammar, and katakana the
foreign words. So every time the script changes, your eye sees where a word starts, and ends.
                                                                          [Idrinkcoffee → I drink coffee; dividers drop]
So, which one did you learn first? Hiragana, katakana, or kanji? Tell me in the comments, and follow for more. またね！

## Fact notes

- Chinese characters reached Japan through contact with China and the Korean Peninsula; written Japanese starts with
  them around the 5th century ("about 1,500 years ago").
- Chinese is an isolating language: words don't inflect.  Japanese verbs do (食べる / 食べた / 食べない).
- Hiragana grew out of cursive man'yōgana: 安 (an) → あ (a).  Katakana came from parts of kanji used by Buddhist scholars
  to annotate texts: the left side of 伊 (i) → イ (i).
- Script changes are a strong cue to word boundaries but not a perfect one (飲 | みます is one word); the dividers are
  drawn between words only.
